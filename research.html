Line of Battle — historical research and modelling decisions

Research checked 2 October 2026. Scope: late eighteenth- and early nineteenth-century sailing combat, especially line-of-battle tactics, gunnery, raking, collision and surrender. The prototype teaches tactical relationships; it is not a reconstruction of a particular ship or battle.

What the sources support

1. Following a leader in line ahead has a historical basis. Period fighting instructions direct ships to take their course from the leading ship and steer in succession. Instructions also require adjacent ships to restore a broken line after a disabled ship drops out. The game's shared wake is an abstraction of those orders, not a literal coupling between ships. Period orders collected by the Navy Records Society, edited by Julian Corbett.

2. Broadside geometry creates an approach-versus-fire tradeoff. Nelson's attacking columns approached an enemy line while exposed to its broadsides; their main batteries could not reply effectively until the ships obtained suitable bearings. Breaking a line creates opportunities but exposes the leading ships. There were some bow and stern guns; the prototype omits those smaller batteries. Royal Museums Greenwich fact sheet, Nelson's surviving 1805 tactical sketch, Niblack's 1902 comparison of sail and steam tactics.

3. Raking means firing along a ship's length. In the Enterprise–Boxer engagement, crossing ahead let Enterprise deliver fire through Boxer's bow along its length, while Boxer could reply only with its limited bow guns. This supports longitudinal deck exposure and an unequal opportunity to return fire. It does not establish a universal damage multiplier. Naval History and Heritage Command, Enterprise versus Boxer.

4. Raking effectiveness depends on more than the angle. Range, shot type and weight, hull construction, what a shot actually traverses, and the state of the crew matter. A narrow end-on silhouette can also be missed. The prototype uses the shot's intersection with the actual hull and the length of its path through the gun deck. The longer path reduces more fighting strength; the previous flat 1.5× hull-damage bonus is removed. Stern vulnerability and rudder damage are qualitative modelling choices, not historical numerical ratios. NHHC's battle account, USS Constitution Museum gun-deck interpretation.

5. Crew losses and damaged guns can defeat a ship before it sinks. Captain Lucas's report from Redoutable describes disabled guns, shattered sides and decks, and extensive casualties before surrender. The report is evidence from one participant, not a neutral measurement of every event. The game therefore tracks hull integrity separately from fighting strength and lets a ship strike its colours while still afloat. Lucas's contemporary report, translated by Fondation Napoléon.

6. Collisions can check progress and foul rigging. Constitution's 1812 log records Guerriere's bowsprit becoming entangled in its mizzen rigging. Such contact could create boarding opportunities and further damage; ships did not pass through one another. The game rejects a proposed move if the solid hull would overlap another, disrupts sailing performance after contact, and permits an automatic backing manoeuvre to clear the obstruction. It does not simulate rigging ropes or boarding. Battle log reproduced by the USS Constitution Museum, museum account of the entangled ships.

7. Backing under sail was possible. Constitution backed its topsails to move sternward during the Cyane–Levant action. Automatic backing after obstruction is inspired by that capability; it is not a claim that every collision historically prompted a standard thirty-five-second procedure. USS Constitution Museum, Cyane and Levant.

8. Wind belongs in a teaching model of sailing tactics. The weather gage meant holding the windward position; beating to windward required successive courses rather than sailing straight into the wind. The Chesapeake account also shows a one-cable formation interval in that particular action. The game's fixed northerly wind and simple speed polar teach the constraint without requiring separate sail controls. Its 110 m centre spacing is deliberately compact and is not presented as the standard historical interval. NHHC, The Pivot Upon Which Everything Turned.

9. Reloading required a multi-step drill and a crew. The museum's educational drill describes about two minutes for trained men to perform the seventeen steps of firing a cannon. That is the game's baseline, with selectable 12×, 24× and 36× time compression and slower reloads as fighting strength declines. Actual rates varied with guns, training, conditions and casualties; two minutes is not asserted to be universal. USS Constitution Museum, Ready? Aim. Fire!.

10. “Out of action” is more accurate than “all ships immediately sink.” Guerriere surrendered, was found unsuitable for towing, and was subsequently destroyed. Likewise, Redoutable surrendered during Trafalgar and sank the next day. The game's struck hulls remain afloat as obstacles; only destroyed hull integrity clears a hull as sunk. This is still a simplified aftermath. Constitution's contemporary log, Royal Museums Greenwich on Redoutable's chronology.

11. A first hull is not always a perfect shot-stopper. Research on Trafalgar describes the danger of shot passing through Redoutable into Temeraire. The game permits round shot with enough residual model energy to continue into another hull, including a friendly one. It does not calculate timber fracture or actual projectile penetration. Michael Duffy, The gunnery at Trafalgar: Training, tactics or temperament?.

12. The battlefield was less legible than a clean tactical chart. Greenwich's interpretation of Pocock's on-site 1794 sketches stresses the dense gun smoke that later paintings often conceal. Perfect information remains an explicit simplification here, preserving the user's preference for strategy and clear geometry. Royal Museums Greenwich, Invincible and Le Juste.

Tactical lessons the game can reasonably demonstrate

Keep a line so neighbouring ships do not mask the guns. Turn across an enemy's bow or stern to obtain a rake, while remembering that the ships following your wake remain exposed. Take account of wind and the space needed for a turn. Avoid colliding with the line you are trying to break. Damaging an opponent's fighting strength, rigging and steering can be more decisive than reducing hull integrity alone. A close-range encounter allows more of a dispersed volley to hit, while a longer-range end-on shot has a smaller silhouette to strike.

Exact sail handling, leeway and currents, sea-state roll, elevation, ammunition choices, powder quality, fires, pumping and repairs, signals, morale, smoke obscuration, boarding, and individual captains' initiative remain outside this prototype. These omissions prevent it from being a complete historical simulator. The original single-line control is retained for simplicity.
